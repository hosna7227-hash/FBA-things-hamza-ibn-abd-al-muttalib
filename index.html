A Companion of the Prophet ﷺ

Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib (RA)

The Sword, the Martyr, and the Shield of the Prophet ﷺ

Hazrat Hamza (RA) was the paternal uncle and foster brother of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, known as the "Lion of Allah" (أسد الله) and the "Chief of the Martyrs" (سید الشهداء).

A formidable warrior and a pillar of strength for the early Muslim community, he was martyred on 23 March 625 CE (7 Shawwal, 3 AH) at the Battle of Uhud (أُحُد). He fought on the front lines wearing an ostrich feather for distinction, wielding two swords to break through the enemy ranks.

His life traces a single arc across three of the defining episodes of the Prophet's ﷺ mission — a dramatic conversion born of honor in Mecca, a decisive duel at Badr, and a martyrdom at Uhud whose aftermath produced one of the most remarkable stories of repentance in the entire Sira.

Early Life & Lineage

I

Birth and Kinship

Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim (RA) was born in Mecca around 570 CE — the same year as the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, which made the two close in age as well as kin. His father, Abd al-Muttalib, was the chief of the Banu Hashim clan and the Prophet's grandfather, making Hamza the Prophet's paternal uncle.

He was also the Prophet's foster brother: both were nursed by the same wet-nurse, Thuwaybah, in infancy — a bond that in Arab custom carried weight as strong as blood kinship.

He is known by the kunya "Abu Ya'la" and, by other accounts, "Abu Umarah," and his mother was Hala bint Wuhayb, herself a cousin of the Prophet's ﷺ own mother Aminah, which drew the two branches of the family closer still. Genealogists trace his line through Abd al-Muttalib ibn Hashim ibn Abd Manaf, placing him among the most senior members of the Banu Hashim in the generation before the Hijrah.

Growing up within Banu Hashim, Hamza was raised alongside the Prophet ﷺ in the household of their grandfather, sharing in the customs of guardianship over the Kaaba and the hospitality duties that gave the clan its standing among the tribes of Quraysh. In his youth he is also counted among the participants of Hilf al-Fudul, the "Pact of the Virtuous" — an alliance of Meccan clans formed to protect the wronged and the defenceless in the city, regardless of tribe, which the Prophet ﷺ himself later praised and said he would still honour even after the coming of Islam. He later married and had several children, among them his daughter Umamah bint Hamza, who is remembered in the Sira for a custody dispute after her father's death that the Prophet ﷺ himself helped settle, ruling that she be raised by her maternal aunt in keeping with the closeness of that bond.

II

Reputation Before Islam

Even before embracing Islam, Hamza was among the most respected men of Quraysh — a skilled hunter, wrestler, and archer known for his physical strength and fierce sense of honor. He carried the epithet "Asad Allah" long before it took on its later religious meaning, earned simply through his standing in combat and the hunt.

He was known to spend much of his time outside the city on hunting expeditions, often returning to circle the Kaaba before going home — a habit that placed him, by providence, right at the center of the event that would change the course of his life.

Contemporaries described him as tall and powerfully built, with a presence that commanded attention even in a gathering of Quraysh's other noblemen. This physical stature, paired with a temperament quick to anger at any perceived insult to his family's honor, is what made the events of that particular afternoon near the Kaaba unfold exactly as they did.

Embracing Islam

III

A Moment of Honor Becomes Faith

Hamza's acceptance of Islam, around 616 CE in the sixth year of Prophethood, is one of the most dramatic conversion accounts in early Islamic history. Returning from a hunting trip, he learned that Abu Jahl had publicly insulted and physically abused the Prophet ﷺ near the Kaaba while Hamza was away.

According to the earliest biographers, it was a freed slave-girl belonging to Abdullah ibn Jud'an who had witnessed the confrontation at the Kaaba that day. As Hamza passed by on his way home, his bow still slung over his shoulder, she recounted to him in detail what Abu Jahl had said and done — that he had cursed the Prophet ﷺ, insulted him, and struck him, and that the Prophet ﷺ had sat in silence and not responded.

Enraged, Hamza went directly to Abu Jahl, struck him with his bow in front of the gathered Quraysh, and declared that he too followed the religion of Muhammad ﷺ, daring anyone present to respond if they dared.

The confrontation took place in full view of the leading men of Quraysh gathered near the Kaaba, and Abu Jahl — though humiliated before his own people — chose not to retaliate, reportedly acknowledging that he had indeed insulted the Prophet ﷺ without cause. The episode instantly became known across Mecca, and Hamza's stature meant the news of his conversion could not be dismissed or hidden.

IV

From Honor to Sincere Belief

What began as an act of tribal honor developed, almost immediately, into sincere faith. Hamza's public allegiance was a turning point for the small Muslim community in Mecca: as one of the most feared and respected men in Quraysh, his conversion gave the early Muslims a measure of protection and standing they had lacked before.

Islamic biographers count his conversion, alongside that of Umar ibn al-Khattab (RA) a short time later, as one of the two events that allowed the Muslims to begin praying openly at the Kaaba rather than in hiding — a shift in the balance of power within Mecca that the Quraysh could no longer ignore.

At the Battle of Badr (624 CE)

V

Single Combat

Hamza's role at Badr cemented his reputation as a peerless warrior. Before the main armies engaged, three champions of Quraysh — Utbah ibn Rabi'ah, his brother Shaybah, and his son al-Walid — stepped forward to demand single combat, and the Prophet ﷺ sent out Hamza, Ubaydah ibn al-Harith, and Ali ibn Abi Talib (RA) to meet them.

In the ritual duels that opened the battle, Hamza faced and killed Utbah ibn Rabi'ah — the father of Hind bint Utbah — a killing that would later fuel her thirst for revenge at Uhud. Ali (RA) killed al-Walid, and Hamza went on to help Ubaydah against Shaybah after Ubaydah was badly wounded, ensuring all three Quraysh champions fell before the general fighting even began.

VI

Lion of Allah

Throughout the battle Hamza fought at the front lines, and Badr is often cited as the moment his title "Lion of Allah" became attached to his name in its religious sense, rather than merely as a warrior's nickname from his pre-Islamic reputation.

The decisive Muslim victory at Badr, against a Quraysh force several times larger, was remembered by the early community as a turning point sent by divine aid, and Hamza's role in the opening duels was recounted as one of its defining moments — which is precisely why his death at Utbah's daughter's hand a year later carried such weight of personal vengeance for the Quraysh.

Accounts of the battle also describe Hamza marking himself out with a distinctive tuft of ostrich feathers tied to his chest, a personal emblem that let both friend and foe recognize him in the chaos of close combat — the same marker he would wear again, with fatal consequence, at Uhud a year later. His courage at Badr, combined with his standing as one of the Prophet's ﷺ closest kinsmen, meant that from this battle onward the Quraysh regarded him not merely as a formidable individual fighter but as a symbol of the young Muslim community's growing strength.

The Circumstances of His Martyrdom

I

The Setup

Hind bint Utbah, wife of Abu Sufyan, sought revenge for the death of her father and uncle, killed by Hamza (RA) in single combat at the Battle of Badr.

She turned to Wahshi ibn Harb, an Abyssinian slave renowned for his skill with a javelin, and promised him his freedom — and, by some accounts, a reward from her own jewelry — if he killed Hazrat Hamza (RA). Wahshi himself later recounted that he had never missed a throw with his javelin, and that Hind's offer was the decisive reason he agreed to march out with the Quraysh army toward Uhud.

II

The Strike

During the battle, Hamza (RA) fought with immense bravery, wielding two swords and felling a number of Quraysh standard-bearers, wearing a distinctive ostrich-feather plume so his position could be recognized by the Muslim ranks.

In the accounts preserved from Wahshi himself, Hamza was at one point engaged in a fierce exchange with a Quraysh fighter named Siba' ibn Abd al-'Uzza, whom Hamza taunted openly on the battlefield, mocking him as the son of a woman who practised circumcision — a deliberate insult meant to provoke and humiliate his opponent in front of both armies. It was in the moments after this exchange, while Hamza's attention was still on the fighting in front of him, that Wahshi found his opening.

While he was occupied in combat, Wahshi — stalking him from behind cover throughout the battle, using rocks and trees to stay hidden as he had planned in advance — waited for a clear line before he threw his javelin, striking Hamza in the abdomen and fatally wounding him. By his own later account, Wahshi did not dare approach openly, knowing that in a direct fight he stood little chance against Hamza; he recalled watching and waiting for a long stretch of the battle before the chance finally presented itself, then retrieving his javelin from the body once he was sure Hamza had fallen, and taking no further part in the fighting that day.

III

The Prophet ﷺ's Grief

After the battle, the bodies of the Muslim martyrs were badly mutilated by the Quraysh — Hind and other Quraysh women disfigured Hamza's body in an act of vengeance, leaving it among the most severely mutilated of all the martyrs of Uhud. Accounts in the Sira describe Hind going so far as to mutilate his liver in her rage, a detail that later Muslim narrators record as the extreme of what wartime vengeance had reached that day.

The state of his uncle's body brought the Prophet ﷺ immense grief; the Companions said they had never seen him weep as deeply as he did for Hazrat Hamza (RA). His sister, Safiyyah bint Abd al-Muttalib (RA) — also the Prophet's aunt — came to the battlefield intending to see the body, and the Prophet ﷺ, wishing to spare her the sight, asked her son Zubayr ibn al-Awwam to intercept her; she insisted she had already heard of the mutilation and could bear it with patience, and was permitted to approach and pray over her brother.

He stood by the body and vowed to retaliate, though revelation soon instructed him to practise patience instead (understood to relate to Qur'an 16:126). Because so many martyrs needed burial and Hamza was brought forward at each subsequent funeral prayer as others were added, his funeral prayer is said to have been repeated around seventy times — more than any other martyr of Uhud. He was buried at the foot of Mount Uhud in Madinah, where his grave remains today within the Uhud Martyrs' Cemetery, alongside his nephew and fellow martyr Mus'ab ibn Umayr (RA).

IV

The Redemption of Wahshi

After Uhud, Wahshi was granted his freedom as promised and lived on in Mecca, but could not forgive himself for what he had done. When the Muslims conquered Mecca in 630 CE, he fled to Ta'if with the remaining pagans there, fearing execution — until he learned that Islam pardons all past sins upon true and sincere repentance, and that the Prophet ﷺ had in fact excluded very few individuals from his general amnesty.

He travelled to Madinah, repented, and embraced Islam before the Prophet ﷺ himself. According to the accounts, when Wahshi identified himself as the killer of Hamza, the Prophet ﷺ, still moved by grief, asked only: "Hide your face from me, and do not let me see you" — for the sight of Wahshi revived his grief over Hamza (RA). Wahshi honoured this and kept out of the Prophet's direct line of sight from then on, never approaching him closely again during his lifetime.

After the Prophet's ﷺ passing, Wahshi joined the Muslim army under Caliph Abu Bakr (RA) in the Battle of Yamama in 634 CE against the forces of the false prophet Musaylimah, seeking redemption. With the very same spear he had used against Hamza, he slew Musaylimah (مسيلمة الكذاب), whose apostate army had risen against the young Muslim state after the Prophet's ﷺ death.

"In my days of ignorance, I killed the best of men — Hamza. In my days of Islam, I killed the worst of men — Musaylimah."

Wahshi is reported to have said he hoped Allah would forgive him through the second killing what the first had made necessary — pairing the worst act of his life with what he considered the best, using the same weapon for both.

Reflected in the words of the Qur'an — Surah Āl ʿImrān, 3:169

"Never regard those slain in the way of Allah as dead. They are alive, sustained in the presence of their Lord, rejoicing in the grace He has granted them."

Legacy

V

Hamza (RA) is remembered in Islamic tradition by two enduring titles: "Asad Allah" (Lion of Allah) for his valor, and "Sayyid al-Shuhada" (Chief of the Martyrs) for the manner and significance of his death — a title by which he is distinguished even among the many other Companions later martyred in the path of Islam.

His story is often paired with Wahshi's later redemption at Yamama as a narrative about the completeness of Islamic forgiveness — that no past sin, however grave, closes the door to sincere repentance, and that the same hand which took the life of the best of men could, through faith, go on to serve the same cause it once opposed.

His grave at the foot of Mount Uhud remains a place visited by pilgrims to Madinah to this day, and his martyrdom is commemorated each year in the retelling of the Battle of Uhud across Islamic gatherings and seerah literature worldwide.

The title "Sayyid al-Shuhada" is traced to a narration attributed to the Prophet ﷺ himself, distinguishing Hamza as the foremost among all who would ever be martyred in the cause of Islam. Later scholars of hadith and sira frequently cite this narration alongside the account of his funeral being repeated some seventy times, treating both as evidence of the singular rank he was given among the martyrs of the early Muslim community — a rank that later generations of Muslims continued to invoke whenever they spoke of sacrifice and steadfastness in the path of faith.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Who was Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib (RA)?

He was the paternal uncle and foster brother of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, renowned as a fierce warrior and known by the titles "Lion of Allah" and "Chief of the Martyrs." He was martyred at the Battle of Uhud in 625 CE.

2. Was Hamza older or younger than the Prophet ﷺ?

They were close in age — some accounts say Hamza was two to four years older — and were foster brothers, nursed by the same woman, Thuwaybah, in infancy.

3. Why did Hamza accept Islam?

He converted after learning Abu Jahl had insulted the Prophet ﷺ in his absence; his initial act was one of defending family honor, which then developed into genuine and lasting faith.

4. Why is he called the "Lion of Allah"?

The title reflects his courage and skill in battle, most famously his role at Badr, where he killed Utbah ibn Rabi'ah in single combat, and at Uhud, where he fought on the front lines wielding two swords.

5. Who killed Hamza (RA), and why?

He was killed by Wahshi ibn Harb, a javelin-thrower hired by Hind bint Utbah, who sought revenge for the deaths of her father and uncle at Hamza's hands during the Battle of Badr.

6. How did the Prophet ﷺ react to his death?

The Prophet ﷺ grieved for Hamza (RA) more visibly than for any other martyr, performed repeated funeral prayers over him, and buried him at the foot of Mount Uhud, where his grave remains today.

7. What happened to Wahshi afterward?

Wahshi later travelled to Madinah, embraced Islam, and was forgiven by the Prophet ﷺ. Years afterward, at the Battle of Yamama, he used the same spear to kill the false prophet Musaylimah, seeking redemption for his earlier act.

8. What was Hamza's role before he became a Muslim?

He was already one of the most respected men of Quraysh, known for hunting, wrestling, and archery. He is also counted among the participants of Hilf al-Fudul, a pre-Islamic pact among Meccan clans formed to defend the wronged and the defenceless.

9. Where is Hamza (RA) buried?

He was buried at the foot of Mount Uhud in Madinah, in what is now known as the Uhud Martyrs' Cemetery, alongside his nephew Mus'ab ibn Umayr (RA) and the other Companions martyred in the same battle.
